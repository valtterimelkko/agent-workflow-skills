# Source Evaluation Guide

A comprehensive guide for evaluating source credibility and quality during deep research.

## Source Quality Hierarchy

### Tier 1: Primary Sources (Highest Credibility)
- Original research papers and datasets
- Official government documents and statistics
- Court records and legal documents
- Firsthand accounts and interviews
- Patent filings
- Technical specifications from manufacturers

**Evaluation Criteria:**
- Direct origin of information
- Verifiable data and methodology
- No intermediary interpretation

### Tier 2: Academic Sources
- Peer-reviewed journal articles
- University press publications
- Conference proceedings
- Dissertations and theses
- Academic books from reputable publishers

**Evaluation Criteria:**
- Peer review status
- Journal impact factor
- Citation count
- Author affiliation and credentials
- Methodology transparency

### Tier 3: Expert Sources
- Publications by recognized authorities
- Industry analyst reports (Gartner, Forrester, etc.)
- Think tank research (with bias awareness)
- Professional association publications
- Expert blogs with demonstrated expertise

**Evaluation Criteria:**
- Author expertise and track record
- Organization reputation
- Transparency about methodology
- Disclosure of funding/conflicts

### Tier 4: Reputable Media
- Established news organizations (NYT, WSJ, BBC, etc.)
- Specialized trade publications
- Long-running magazines with editorial standards
- Public broadcasters

**Evaluation Criteria:**
- Editorial standards and corrections policy
- Reporter expertise on topic
- Use of original reporting vs. aggregation
- Separation of news and opinion

### Tier 5: Industry Sources
- Company whitepapers and reports
- Industry association publications
- Corporate blogs
- Product documentation

**Evaluation Criteria:**
- Potential bias (promotional content)
- Technical accuracy
- Transparency about limitations
- Independence of analysis

### Tier 6: Community Sources (Use with Caution)
- Forums (Reddit, Stack Exchange)
- Social media discussions
- User reviews
- Wikipedia (as starting point only)

**Evaluation Criteria:**
- Community moderation quality
- Presence of citations
- Consensus vs. outliers
- Need for verification

## Credibility Assessment Framework

### The SIFT Method

1. **STOP**: Pause and consider if you trust this source
2. **INVESTIGATE THE SOURCE**: Find out who created this and why
3. **FIND BETTER COVERAGE**: Look for trusted reporting on the claim
4. **TRACE CLAIMS**: Find the original source of information

### LATERAL READING

Before reading vertically (through the article), read laterally (across other sources):
- Search for the website/publication on Wikipedia
- Check fact-checking sites (Snopes, FactCheck, etc.)
- Look for reviews or discussions about the source
- Check media bias charts

### CREDIBILITY CHECKLIST

For each source, answer:

**Authority:**
- [ ] Who is the author? What are their credentials?
- [ ] What organization published this?
- [ ] What is the domain expertise?

**Accuracy:**
- [ ] Can claims be verified?
- [ ] Are there citations/references?
- [ ] Is there a corrections policy?
- [ ] Has it been fact-checked?

**Objectivity:**
- [ ] What is the purpose of this source?
- [ ] Is there evident bias?
- [ ] Is funding/sponsorship disclosed?
- [ ] Are alternative viewpoints presented?

**Currency:**
- [ ] When was this published?
- [ ] Has it been updated?
- [ ] Is the information still relevant?
- [ ] Are there newer sources on this topic?

**Coverage:**
- [ ] Is the topic covered comprehensively?
- [ ] Are limitations acknowledged?
- [ ] Is the scope appropriate?

## Red Flags

Watch for these warning signs:

### Content Red Flags
- Sensationalized headlines
- ALL CAPS or excessive punctuation
- No author attribution
- No publication date
- Emotional language without evidence
- Conspiracy theories presented as fact
- Claims that seem too good/bad to be true

### Source Red Flags
- No "About" page or contact information
- Domain mimicking legitimate sites
- Recently registered domain (check whois)
- No references or citations
- Excessive advertising
- Known disinformation sites

### Authorship Red Flags
- No verifiable credentials
- Pseudonyms without transparency
- Conflicts of interest not disclosed
- Track record of false claims
- Lack of expertise in topic area

## Domain-Specific Evaluation

### Scientific/Medical Sources
- PubMed indexing
- Impact factor
- Study design (RCT > observational > case study)
- Sample size
- Conflict of interest disclosures
- Replication status

### Technical/Software Sources
- Official documentation
- Community adoption
- Maintainer reputation
- Update frequency
- Security audit status
- Version currency

### Business/Financial Sources
- SEC filings (primary)
- Audited financial statements
- Analyst credentials
- Track record of predictions
- Disclosure of positions
- Independence from companies covered

### News Sources
- Editorial standards
- Corrections policy
- Pulitzer/other awards
- Reporter expertise
- Original reporting vs. aggregation
- Separation of news/opinion

## Bias Assessment

### Types of Bias to Consider

1. **Confirmation Bias**: Source only presents evidence supporting one view
2. **Selection Bias**: Cherry-picked data or examples
3. **Funding Bias**: Results favoring funding source
4. **Political Bias**: Consistent slant toward political positions
5. **Commercial Bias**: Promotional rather than informational
6. **Recency Bias**: Over-emphasizing recent events
7. **Availability Bias**: Over-relying on easily accessible information

### Assessing Balance

A quality source should:
- Present multiple viewpoints
- Acknowledge limitations and uncertainties
- Distinguish between facts and opinions
- Provide context for claims
- Cite opposing evidence

## Quick Reference: Credibility Scoring

| Factor | +1 | 0 | -1 |
|--------|----|---|----|
| Author credentials | Verified expert | Unclear | No credentials |
| References | Extensive citations | Some | None |
| Publication | Tier 1-2 | Tier 3-4 | Tier 5-6 |
| Date | Current/relevant | Older but valid | Outdated |
| Cross-verification | Confirmed by 3+ sources | Partially confirmed | Contradicted |
| Bias disclosure | Transparent | Unclear | Hidden agenda |

**Score Interpretation:**
- 5-6: Highly credible
- 3-4: Credible with caveats
- 0-2: Use with caution
- Negative: Avoid or verify extensively

## Summary

When in doubt:
1. Prefer primary sources
2. Verify through lateral reading
3. Check multiple sources for key claims
4. Document your evaluation process
5. Be transparent about limitations
